5  Common Discrete Random Variables

5.1 Introduction

Now that we’ve looked at general properties and uses of discrete random variables, we’ll investigate some types of discrete random variables that are often useful.

5.2 Definitions, Exercises, and Theorems

We’ll start with a result that we will not prove at this time. The first part is the other aspect of what it means for expectation to be linear. The second part is a useful fact about variance.

Theorem 5.1 Given two random variables \(X_1\) and \(X_2\), \(E[X_1 + X_2] = E[X_1] + E[X_2]\).

If random variables \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) are independent, then \(\mathrm{Var}(X_1 + X_2) = \mathrm{Var}(X_1) + \mathrm{Var}(X_2).\)

We haven’t formally defined independence for random variables yet. For now, we can use this to represent independent trials of the same random process.

Now we’ll move into the types of random variables. The first two will be familiar from our discussion of counting.

Definition 5.1: Bernoulli Random Variable

A Bernoulli random variable with parameter \(p\), written as \(X \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)\), has successes (with \(X=1\)) and failures (With \(X=0\)) and a probability mass function given by \[P_X(k) = \begin{cases} 1-p & k=0 \\ p & k=1 \end{cases}.\]

Definition 5.2: Binomial Random Variable

A binomial random variable with parameters \(n\) and \(p\), written as \(X \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n,p)\), is \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials, with \(P_X(k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}\).

You already proved this expression for the PMF of a binomial random variable in Theorem 3.3.

Exercise 5.1 Find the expectation and variance of a Bernoulli random variable in terms of \(p\).

Exercise 5.2 A basketball player who makes 90% of free throw attempts has 8 attempts in a game. What is the probability of this player making exactly 7 of those free throws? 7 or more?

Exercise 5.3 A communication system consists of \(n\) components, each of which will independently function with probability \(p\). The total system will be able to operate if at least half of its components function. For what values of \(p\) is a 5-component system more likely to operate than a 3-component system? (Hint: write the inequality that would let you find this, and then feel free to use a computer to solve it.)

The next lemma will help us find the expectation of a binomial random variable.

Theorem 5.2 Prove that the expectation of a binomial random variable is \(np\) and that the variance is \(np(1-p)\). (Hint: use Theorem 5.1.)

Binomial random variables aren’t the only family that we can build based on Bernoulli trials.

Definition 5.3: Geometric Random Variable

A geometric random variable with parameter \(p\), or \(X \sim \mathrm{Geometric}(p)\), is the total number of independent Bernoulli trials to achieve the first success. It has probability mass function \(P_X(k) = p(1-p)^{k-1}\).

Exercise 5.4 Consider the basketball player from Exercise 5.2. What is the probability that it would take that player exactly 2 attempts to make their first free throw of the game? exactly 3 attempts?

Exercise 5.5 Use the sum of a geometric series to show that the geometric random variable’s PMF does indeed sum to 1 over all possible values of \(k\).

Theorem 5.3 For a geometric random variable \(X\), prove that \(E[X] = \frac{1}{p}\). (Hint: One way to approach this is to take a derivative with respect to \(r\) of a geometric series. Another way is to split the sum into many similar sums that start at different values.)

Theorem 5.4 For a geometric random variable \(X\), prove that \(\mathrm{Var}(x) = \frac{1-p}{p^2}\). (Hint: one approach is to use that \(E[X^2] = E[X(X-1)] + E[X]\) and use the second derivative of a geometric series.)

Exercise 5.6 Go back once more to the basketball player from Exercise 5.2. How many attempts \(X\) would we expect them to need to make their first free throw of the game? What is the variance of \(X\)?

We’re not always interested in just the first success. We can expand this idea a bit more.

Definition 5.4: Negative Binomial Random Variable

A negative binomial random variable or Pascal random variable, \(X \sim \mathrm{NB}(m,p)\), is the number of independent Bernoulli trials with probability of success \(p\) until there have been \(m\) successes.

Theorem 5.5 Explain why, for \(X \sim \mathrm{NB}(m,p)\), \(P_X(k) = \binom{k-1}{m-1} p^m (1-p)^{k-m}\).

Exercise 5.7 What’s the probability that we need to roll a fair six-sided die 10 times to get three rolls of 2?

Prove only the expected value portion of the following:

Theorem 5.6 The expected value of a negative binomial random variable \(\mathrm{NB}(m,p)\) is \(\frac{m}{p}\), and the variance is \(\frac{m(1-p)}{p^2}\). (These should seem related to the expectation and variance of a geometric variable!)

Exercise 5.8 A company needs to hire four robot operators, and they will hire the first four applicants that are suitable. The probability that any given applicant is suitable is 0.8. Find the probability that the company interviews more than 6 applicants before filling all the jobs.

We can also use the success/failure structure a different way:

Definition 5.5: Hypergeometric Random Variable

A hypergeometric random variable, \(X \sim \mathrm{Hypergeometric}(b,r,k)\), is the number of successes in an experiment where \(k\) items are chosen without replacement from a group of \(b+r\) things, \(b\) of them successes and \(r\) of them failures.

Theorem 5.7 Explain why, for \(X\) a hypergeometric random variable, \(P_X(z) = \dfrac{\binom{b}{z}\binom{r}{k-z}}{\binom{b+r}{k}}\).

Exercise 5.9 A process known as capture-recapture is often used to estimate animal populations or density. Suppose that 20 ocelots live in a particular wetland area. You initially capture, mark, and release 5 of them. A few months later, you capture and then release 6 ocelots. What is the probability that none of the ocelots were already marked?

We can also think about how many times something occurs in a given amount of time or space as opposed to a certain number of discrete trials.

Definition 5.6: Poisson Random Variable

A Poisson (pronounced pwah-sawn) random variable \(X \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)\) tells us how many times a certain event occurs in a given interval of time/space if \(\lambda\) is how often it occurs on average. The probability mass function is \(P_X(k) = \dfrac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!}.\)

Exercise 5.10 Suppose that the number of emails I receive on weekends can be modeled as a Poisson distribution with \(\lambda = \frac{1}{30}\) emails per minute. What is the probability that I receive no emails between 8am and noon on Sunday? At most two emails?

Exercise 5.11 Suppose that the number of typos in one of these chapters has a Poisson distribution with parameter \(\lambda = \frac{1}{2}\). Calculate the probability that there is at least one error in this chapter.

Theorem 5.8 Prove that for \(X \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)\), the expectation \(E[X] = \lambda\) and the variance \(\mathrm{Var}[X] = \lambda\). (Note: look out for the Taylor series expansion of \(e^\lambda\).)
