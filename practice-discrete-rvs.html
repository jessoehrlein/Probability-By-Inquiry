6  Practice with Discrete Random Variables

6.1 Introduction

We’ve now seen a variety of discrete random variables, so we’ll do some practice with each family of variables and then look at some problems where we need to first determine the appropriate distribution or tools to use.

6.2 Definitions, Exercises, and Theorems

Exercise 6.1 There’s a famous story in probability & statistics history about a woman who claimed that she could tell whether a cup of tea had been prepared with tea in the cup first or milk in the cup first. She was given eight cups of tea, four prepared with tea first and four with milk first, and asked to identify the four prepared with milk first. Assume that she was actually just guessing. Explain why her number of correct guesses is a hypergeometric variable, and find the probability that at least 3 of her guesses were correct.

Exercise 6.2 A student is applying for internships and has a probability of 0.4 of being invited for a phone interview for each job independently. Explain why the number of jobs the student applies for before being invited for an interview is a geometric random variable, and find the expected number of jobs the student would need to apply to in order to get one interview.

Exercise 6.3 An airline overbooks a flight. The airplane has seats for 140 passengers, and 143 passengers have booked the flight. Suppose that each passenger independently has a probability of 0.9 of showing up for the flight. Explain why the number of passengers who show up is a binomial random variable, and find the probability that there will be seats for everyone who shows up.

Exercise 6.4 Suppose the number of hits a web site receives in any time interval is a Poisson random variable. A particular site gets on average 5 hits per second. What is the probability that, in a particular two second period, there will be at most 1 hit?

Exercise 6.5 About 3% of all operating roller coasters are wooden roller coasters. Suppose a coaster enthusiast rides roller coasters at random. Use the negative binomial distribution to determine the probability that the 48th wooden roller coaster the enthusiast rides is the 230th roller coaster they ride overall? (Bonus: what might be unrealistic about the assumptions needed in this problem?)

Exercise 6.6 A company has purchased a policy that will compensate for the loss of revenue due to severe weather events. The policy pays $1000 for each severe weather event in a year after the first two such events in that year. The number of severe weather events per year has a Poisson distribution with mean 1. Calculate the expected amount paid to this company in one year.

Exercise 6.7 A patient suffered brain damage that caused a loss of vision on the left side of her visual field. A researcher showed two cards to this patient. Each card showed a simple line drawing of a house. However, one of the drawings showed flames coming out of the left side of the house. The researcher shuffled the two cards, placed them down on a table with one card above the other, and asked the patient which house she would rather live in. The patient replied that this was a silly question because “they are the same.” The researcher asked her to choose anyway. The cards were shuffled and placed on the table a total of 17 times. The patient chose the non-burning house in 14 of those 17 showings. What is the probability that the patient would have chosen the non-burning house at least 14 out of 17 times by chance?

Exercise 6.8 A purchaser of electrical components buys them in lots of size 10. It is his policy to inspect 3 components randomly from a lot and to accept the lot only if all 3 are nondefective. If 30 percent of the lots have 4 defective components and 70 percent have only 1, what proportion of the lots do we expect the purchaser to reject?

Exercise 6.9 Suppose you play a one-player casino game in which a fair coin is flipped until a tails appears. For the \(n\)th flip, the pot is \(2^n\), and you win the pot when a tails appears. A particular player is willing to play the game if what they would be expected to win is greater than or equal to what they pay to play. How much would they be willing to pay for this game?

Exercise 6.10 Now imagine that the casino has a finite amount of cash, say \(2^{26}\) dollars (around $70 million), so they modify the game. If the first 26 flips are heads, the game ends and the player wins \(2^{26}\) dollars. How much should the player from the last exercise now be willing to pay to play?

Exercise 6.11 On average over 1980-2019, the average number of hurricanes to make landfall in the US as hurricanes per year was 1.55. In 2020, 6 hurricanes made landfall. Assuming the 1980-2019 rate, what is the probability of at least 6 hurricanes making landfall in the US in a single year?

Exercise 6.12 You win each game you play with probability \(p\), and you plan to play five games. However, if you win the fifth game, you continue playing until you lose. Find the expected number of games you play.

Exercise 6.13 An urn contains \(n\) balls labeled 1 through \(n\). You draw out \(m\) balls one at a time with replacement. Let \(X\) be the maximum of the \(m\) chosen numbers. Find the probability \(P(X \leq k)\), and then use that expression to find \(P(X = k)\).

Exercise 6.14 Suppose that the population of sea otters in a region is Poisson distributed, with an average of 1.04 adult sea otters per square kilometer. A team of researchers survey a 5 square kilometer area in the region. What is the probability that there are fewer than three adult sea otters in the area?

Exercise 6.15 In a certain lottery, each ticket has an independent probability \(p\) of being a winning ticket. Jo buys three tickets, thinking that this will triple her chances of winning. Show that the probability of at least one of her tickets being a winning ticket is \(p^3 - 3p^2 + 3p\), and show that this did not quite triple her chances of winning compared to buying only one ticket.

Exercise 6.16 An insurance policy on an electrical device pays a benefit of $4000 if the devices fails during the first year. The amount of the benefit decreases by $1000 each successive year until it reaches 0. If the device has not failed by the beginning of any given year, the probability of failure during that year is 0.4. Calculate the expected benefit and the variance of the benefit under this policy.

Exercise 6.17 Mike has built a tall tower in West Texas, so he can see for a long ways. He sits in the tower and look for armadillos. Suppose that he sees on average four armadillos per hour. What’s the probability that in a one hour period Mike sees six armadillos? What’s the probability that in a two hour period he sees ten armadillos?

Exercise 6.18 Five engineering students want to build a water curtain, a type of fountain in which water falls vertically to make patterns. The team orders 100 solenoid valves. The valves are cheap, so 20 of the valves in the box don’t work. The students need 60 for their project. What’s the probability that they pull out 70 to test and 60 of them work?

Exercise 6.19 A duck boat tour operator has a bus that can accommodate 35 tourists. The operator knows that tourists may not show up, so they sell 36 tickets. The probability that an individual tourist will not show up is 0.03, independent of all other tourists. Each ticket costs $50 and is non-refundable. If a tourist shows up and a seat is not available, the tour operator has to pay $100 to the tourist. Calculate the expected revenue of the operator.

Exercise 6.20 Sudden stratospheric warmings are phenomena that sometimes occur about 30 km above the Arctic surface in winter that can influence surface climate in Eastern North America and Europe for up to two months. There are an average of 0.62 sudden stratospheric warmings per year. There were only two of these events in the 1990s. What is the probability of at most two sudden stratospheric warmings occurring in a decade?

Exercise 6.21 It is possible but somewhat rare for multiple sudden stratospheric warmings to occur in a year. For simplicity, some studies look at the number of winters in which a sudden stratospheric warming occurred instead of number of events. Sudden stratospheric warmings occur in about 53% of winters. There was only one winter in the 1990s with a sudden stratospheric warming. What is the probability of at most one winter with a sudden stratospheric warming occurring in a decade?

Exercise 6.22 The number of claims \(X\) on a health insurance policy is a random variable with \(E[X^2] = 61\) and \(E[(X-1)^2] = 47\). Calculate the standard deviation of the number of claims.

Exercise 6.23 Ten cards from a deck of playing cards are in a box: two diamonds, three spades, and five hearts. Two cards are randomly selected without replacement. Calculate the variance of the number of diamonds selected, given that no spade is selected.

Exercise 6.24 A representative of a market research firm contacts consumers by phone in order to conduct surveys. The specific consumer contacted by each phone call is randomly determined, and the probability that a call produces a completed survey is 0.25. Calculate the probability that more than three phone calls are required to produce one completed survey.

Exercise 6.25 The number of traffic tickets a driver receives this year is Poisson distributed. The driver’s probability of receiving no tickets is \(e^{-1.5}\). Calculate the probability that the driver receives at least four tickets this year, given that the driver receives at least one ticket.

Exercise 6.26 Prove that the geometric distribution is . That is, if \(X\) is geometrically distributed and \(n,m\) are integers greater than 0, show that \(P(X > n+m | X > m) = P(X > n)\).

Exercise 6.27 The operating cost of a new claims system is modeled by a random variable \(X\) with variance 50. In its second year of use, inflation of 3% and an additional fixed maintenance cost of 2.5 increase the operating cost of the system. Calculate the variance of the operating cost of the system in its second year of use.

Exercise 6.28 A group of 17 people in a study on lung cancer consists of 3 heavy smokers, 4 light smokers, and 10 non-smokers. Six people from the group are chosen at random for a new treatment. Calculate the probability that three of those chosen are non-smokers.
